# Ecology Letters cover letter — draft v1

Dear Editors,

We submit **“Assembly history is stored through contrasting structural channels in ecological communities”** for consideration as a Letter in *Ecology Letters*.

Priority effects are well established: arrival order can alter community composition, ecosystem processes and host-associated function. Our manuscript asks a different question. **When an experimentally generated historical effect persists, where in the present community is that history actually retained?**

We address this question by reanalysing two independent randomized arrival-order experiments—a plant phyllosphere microbiome and a grassland community—under a common, design-respecting framework. In both experiments, assembly history remains strongly visible in final community composition but is close to its randomization baseline in another state measured on the same experimental units: host rust disease in the microbiome and belowground root distribution in the grassland.

The key analysis then separates two components of composition without changing the underlying abundance data. For each experimental unit, we remove ecological identity by sorting the exact same abundance values while preserving response dimensionality, experimental units, distance metric and history model. This produces contrasting results. Fungal assembly history remains essentially intact after taxon names are erased, showing that the microbiome stores a strong historical signature in unlabeled abundance architecture. In the grassland, most retained history disappears when functional-group identities are removed, indicating a much more identity-dependent structural legacy.

We believe this identity-stripping comparison is the manuscript's main conceptual contribution. It moves the priority-effects question from whether history matters to **which structural component of the present carries the historical legacy**.

We also report a prospective falsification that we consider important to the strength of the paper. A biologically appealing first-arriver-role transformation initially appeared to explain the cross-system contrast. Because that transformation used the observed treatment to choose a response coordinate, we prospectively enumerated every equally complex placebo mapping. The biologically correct mapping ranked only 53rd of 120 in the microbiome and last of six in the grassland. We therefore reject that mechanism rather than preserve it as a post hoc story.

Subsequent prospectively frozen tests within the microbiome localize a narrower result: Alternaria arrival history has the largest leverage on identity-free abundance architecture; that leverage is concentrated more strongly in rank-1 dominance than in lower-rank structure; and the dominance shift is positive in 11 of 12 host genotypes. We stop the inference at this structural result. Reconciliation with the source experiment shows that the archive lacks the absolute-abundance and interaction-level measurements required to distinguish niche pre-emption, niche modification, intrinsic competitive ability or host-mediated effects.

The manuscript is approximately 4,000 words of main text and is designed as a concise general-ecology Letter. It does not claim that priority effects, compositional–functional decoupling or dominance effects are themselves new. Instead, it introduces and empirically decomposes the **structural location of historical contingency in the present state**, with two independent experiments, prospective hypothesis tests, an explicit failed mechanism test, and a defined mechanistic boundary.

We believe the work fits *Ecology Letters* because it uses existing experiments to test a new general ecological question, produces contrasting but interpretable outcomes across microbial and grassland communities, and yields a framework that can be applied to other historical-contingency experiments without requiring a new statistical method.

Thank you for considering the manuscript.

Sincerely,
